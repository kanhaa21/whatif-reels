# Sources: What if your lift cable snapped on the 40th floor?

Checked 20 Nov 2026.

| Claim in the reel | Source |
| --- | --- |
| A traction lift hangs on 4 to 8 steel cables (ropes), and each one alone can hold the car | HowStuffWorks, "What if You Were on an Elevator and the Cable Broke?": "multiple cables -- between four and eight total", "just one cable is usually enough" (https://science.howstuffworks.com/science-vs-myth/everyday-myths/question730.htm). Live Science: "Each of the four to eight steel cables in a traction elevator is strong enough by itself to hold the car" (https://www.livescience.com/33445-how-survive-falling-elevator.html). Science ABC: "four to eight steel hoist ropes" (https://www.scienceabc.com/innovation/how-safe-are-you-in-an-elevator) |
| If the car goes down too fast, a speed governor trips the safety brakes, which grab the guide rails | HowStuffWorks: "When the governor spins too fast, the centrifugal force activates the braking system", "Some safeties clamp the rails". Live Science: "the governor activates brakes on the elevator's travel rails". Science ABC: the safeties "wedge against the steel guide rails" |
| A shock absorber (an oil-filled piston, "oil buffer") sits at the bottom of the shaft | HowStuffWorks: "a built-in shock absorber at the bottom of the shaft — typically a piston in an oil-filled cylinder". Science ABC: "a hydraulic buffer at the bottom of the shaft". (The reel only says it is there; Live Science notes buffers are not designed to catch a free-falling car, so the reel does not claim the buffer saves you.) |
| Jumping at the last second removes only a few km/h | Live Science: "the best speed reduction you could hope for would be 2 or 3 mph" (about 3 to 5 km/h). West Texas A&M (Dr Christopher Baird), assuming a 7 mph (about 11 km/h) best human jump: from 165 ft the impact speed falls only "from 72 mph to 65 mph" (http://www.wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/2012/12/05/if-im-on-an-elevator-that-breaks-loose-and-plummets-down-the-shaft-can-i-avoid-harm-by-jumping-at-the-last-second/). The Conversation also shows a perfect jump barely helps (https://theconversation.com/ive-always-wondered-if-youre-in-a-falling-elevator-can-you-really-save-yourself-by-jumping-99597) |
| "About 170 km/h in about 5 seconds" if nothing stopped it from the 40th floor | Our calculation. A storey is about 3 m (The Conversation uses 3 m per storey), so the 40th floor is about 117 to 120 m up. Free fall: v = sqrt(2gh) = sqrt(2 x 9.81 x 117) = 47.9 m/s = about 172 km/h; t = sqrt(2h/g) = about 4.9 s. Air resistance would lower this slightly, so the reel says "about". |
| What to do: press the alarm, stay inside, wait; once stopped, inside the lift is the safest place | Safety+Health magazine (National Safety Council), quoting the Elevator Escalator Safety Foundation: "Use the elevator's alarm button or emergency contact button to get help", "Stay in a stuck elevator – don't attempt to pry open the doors", "The inside of an elevator is the safest place to be while the elevator is stopped" (https://www.safetyandhealthmagazine.com/articles/16645-the-dos-and-donts-of-elevator-safety). Science ABC: "The golden rule if you are ever caught inside is simply to stay put." |

Dropped: body-position advice (lie flat vs bend your knees). Live Science says lie flat; The Conversation says that is "a terrible idea". The sources disagree, so the reel does not give it.
Dropped: real incidents (2018 Chicago cable break, 1945 Empire State Building) because of the "no current news / real people" rule and to keep the reel short.
